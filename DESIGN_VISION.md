# Seven Days — Design Vision

## Core premise

**Seven Days** is a harmless prank-horror experience built around one simple idea:

Nothing bad is going to happen to the user. The joke is getting them to spend seven days wondering whether something will.

Seven Days never needs to threaten the user or explain what the countdown means. Its central pitch is deliberately ambiguous:

> **Seven Days — the app that lets you know exactly how much time you have left.**

The countdown is the threat. The user's imagination supplies the consequences.

---

## 1. The gift

The experience begins before the app is installed.

The recipient receives an email saying that somebody has gifted them Seven Days. This framing is used even when somebody buys the app for themselves.

The email is deliberately short and impersonal:

> Hi,
>
> Your friend **[NAME]** gifted you a copy of the **Seven Days** app, the app that lets you know exactly how much time you have left.
>
> Thanks,  
> The Team

The email includes a normal store/install link and a gift activation mechanism such as a QR code.

The activation/security implementation is secondary to the experience. The important purpose of onboarding is to establish the recipient, confirm the information the experience legitimately needs, and obtain explicit permissions before anything strange begins.

---

## 2. Activation

After installation, the recipient completes an ordinary-looking gift activation/onboarding process.

This can include legitimate confirmation of email and phone information where required, notification permission, and any other communication permissions that the final platform implementation legitimately supports.

The onboarding should feel mundane rather than spooky.

When the user completes the final Submit/Activate action, Seven Days begins.

---

## 3. First contact and the real start time

Immediately after final activation, Seven Days makes its first contact through the strongest appropriate legitimate channel available to the app.

The content is simply:

> **Seven Days.**

No explanation.

**This moment starts the seven-day countdown.**

The countdown does not begin when the timer is eventually revealed. The configuration sequence, false installation, CRT sequence, and video are already consuming the user's seven days.

---

## 4. The installation that already happened

The installed app opens into what appears to be a mundane post-install configuration/update procedure.

It claims to be downloading configuration files or completing installation and displays a throbber. The apparent progress does not actually complete; it waits until the user touches the screen.

That touch produces an in-experience warning:

> **For your protection, your phone is preventing this app from installing.**
>
> **[INSTALL ANYWAY]**

There is no alternative choice within this fictional interface.

The gag is that the apparent protection mechanism is warning the user away from Seven Days while simultaneously giving them only one useful option: override the protection.

This is theatrical presentation inside an already legitimately installed app, not an actual device-security action.

---

## 5. Install Anyway

Pressing **INSTALL ANYWAY** is the hard transition from mundane onboarding into the cursed experience.

A bizarre sound plays and the normal interface physically melts away, inspired by the classic *Doom* screen-melt transition but rendered smoothly at modern phone resolution.

The interface does not merely fade or slide away. It loses structural integrity and collapses off the display.

When the melt completes, the screen is black.

---

## 6. The dot and the television

At the exact center of the black screen is a tiny white dot.

It grows.

The dot becomes a small rounded rectangle. As it approaches, white/pink-noise static becomes audible and increases with its apparent proximity.

Eventually the shape resolves into the illuminated screen of an old black-and-white CRT television emerging from the darkness.

The television continues approaching until its screen is clearly visible.

Then the video begins.

---

## 7. The poison-pen video

The video has no story and provides no useful explanation.

It is audiovisual poison-pen material: shapes, patterns, visual noise, strange and grotesque imagery, shocking individual frames, uncomfortable compositions, abrupt transitions, and disturbing or inexplicable sound.

Nothing develops into a conventional narrative. There is no creature whose rules can be learned and no exposition explaining what Seven Days supposedly does.

The user should finish it without understanding anything more than they did before it started.

The video is called directly into the Seven Days experience. It is not presented as an external link or an ordinary public video the recipient is directed elsewhere to watch.

When it finishes, the screen returns to black.

---

## 8. The countdown reveal

The app locks its own presentation to landscape and finally reveals the countdown.

Against black are large frightening red numerals.

There is no **DAYS** label and no explanatory paragraph. The user already knows that Seven Days is supposed to show them how much time they have left.

Because the clock began at first contact, the initial display is already below seven complete days. Everything the user just experienced consumed real countdown time.

---

## 9. The timer is the monster

The underlying countdown is honest. There is one real target time.

Seven Days may behave strangely around the clock, but the actual remaining time remains real.

The presentation can occasionally do things the user does not expect from a countdown timer, including transient digit corruption, glitches, brief visual desynchronization followed by correction, strange frames, unexpected graphical elements, typography changes, inexplicable sounds, or unusual responses to interaction.

The governing rule is:

> **Seven Days may lie visually. It does not lie mathematically.**

Usually opening Seven Days simply shows the timer. Sometimes the timer does something strange.

That uncertainty is part of the experience.

---

## 10. The escape

When the countdown is revealed, Seven Days offers its fictional way to stop it:

> **To stop the timer, gift this app to a friend, or someone you don't like.**
>
> **[FRIEND] [NOT FRIEND] [NO THANKS, I'LL WAIT]**

Friend and Not Friend both pass the Seven Days experience to another person through the legitimate gifting flow. The recipient must deliberately accept/install/activate their own copy; Seven Days does not secretly enroll another person.

The third choice is intentionally funny: **NO THANKS, I'LL WAIT.**

Wait for what?

Seven Days never says.

---

## 11. Seven days of gleeful pestering

The countdown is not merely something the user voluntarily checks for a week. Seven Days periodically comes looking for them.

Several times per day, at irregular intervals, Seven Days produces events through legitimate communication capabilities available to the experience.

The primary mechanism is app notifications, potentially supplemented by other properly authorized channels supported by the final implementation.

Events can be:

- unsettling
- cryptic
- satirical
- absurdly cheerful
- apparently corrupted
- nearly empty
- inexplicable
- corporate
- grotesque
- mundane in a suspicious way

A notification can arrive with a bizarre custom Seven Days sound only for the actual notification to be blank, glitched, cryptic, or almost useless.

The events should not follow an obvious linear scare escalation. Something deeply creepy can be followed by something stupidly cheerful or corporate. Unpredictability matters more than steadily increasing scariness.

The desired ordinary-life moment is simple: the user is on a bus, driving to the beach, eating lunch, or otherwise not thinking about Seven Days when their phone suddenly emits some horrible unfamiliar sound. They look and discover that Seven Days sent them something strange or contentless.

Nothing else happens.

Seven Days has succeeded because the user remembered the timer.

---

## 12. Returning to the clock

Touching a Seven Days notification opens the app and normally returns the user to the landscape countdown.

The number is smaller now.

The gifting escape remains available.

Seven Days does not need to explain why it just made the user's phone produce a bizarre sound or display a corrupted notification. The clock is the explanation.

The weekly rhythm becomes:

**Something weird happens → the user checks Seven Days → less time remains → life continues.**

---

## 13. Tone

Seven Days has two overlapping voices.

The actual countdown experience is played straight. It does not constantly wink at the user and announce that everything is a joke.

Around that experience is an absurdly cheerful consumer-product voice. Seven Days behaves as though an unexplained countdown toward zero is a perfectly normal service.

Its language can enthusiastically describe a thrilling countdown timer, knowing exactly how much time the user has left, helpful reminders that time continues to pass, and convenient gifting to friends or people the user does not like.

Seven Days does **not** need to say:

> YOU ARE GOING TO DIE.

Instead it effectively says:

> Here is exactly how much time you have left. We hope you're enjoying the experience.

---

## 14. Zero

Eventually the countdown reaches zero.

Nothing bad happens.

Seven Days does not harm the user, their device, their data, or anybody else.

After completion, Seven Days sends another notification. It can be blank, consistent with the strange notifications the user has learned to receive during the week.

Touching it opens Seven Days as usual, except the countdown has been replaced with:

# YOU SURVIVED!

with:

**[PLAY AGAIN] [GIFT TO A FRIEND] [GIFT TO A NON-FRIEND]**

That is the central punchline.

The user spent seven days wondering what happens at zero.

The answer is nothing.

Congratulations. You survived.

---

## 15. Completed state

The special completion reveal does not need to replay forever.

After it has been viewed, Seven Days settles into its ordinary completed state, retaining replay and gifting options.

The prank is complete.

---

## 16. The thing that does not exist

Approximately one completed run in one thousand contains an additional undocumented event immediately before the canonical completed state.

The user sees:

# YOU DIED!

Nothing bad happens.

The phone does not change behavior, the user's data is untouched, and the user is fine.

This is **not an alternate ending**. Every Seven Days countdown still ultimately ends with **YOU SURVIVED!**

The YOU DIED screen is an extremely rare transient pre-ending event.

It should not be documented by Seven Days. There is no achievement, counter, collectible, rarity listing, or official acknowledgement for finding it.

At a 1-in-1,000 probability per completed run, the expected wait for one occurrence is 1,000 completed runs: 7,000 days, or roughly 19.2 years, if one person attempted consecutive full runs. Even after 1,000 independent runs, occurrence is not guaranteed.

It is intended as folklore, not reasonable completionist content.

---

## 17. Don't take a screenshot

The rare event contains one final prank.

The user sees **YOU DIED!** and may naturally attempt to capture a screenshot.

Where the platform technically permits Seven Days to detect that action appropriately, the **first screenshot attempt is the trap**.

Seven Days glitches hard.

The rare state disappears and resolves into:

# YOU SURVIVED!

The user's first attempt has destroyed the one-view event rather than preserving useful evidence. If they try to take another screenshot, it is too late: the screen now genuinely says **YOU SURVIVED!**

The governing joke is:

> **Attempting to preserve the evidence destroys the evidence.**

Exact screenshot-detection behavior remains subject to platform/API feasibility; this section specifies the intended experience.

A user may still photograph the phone with another device. That is acceptable and even useful to the folklore because external photos and videos can always be disputed as edited, mocked up, or produced by a fake build.

---

## 18. The urban legend and official answer

Seven Days should never provide official confirmation of a secret YOU DIED ending.

The official answer can remain completely truthful:

> **There is no YOU DIED ending. Every Seven Days countdown ends with YOU SURVIVED.**

That is true because YOU DIED is not an ending. It is an alleged transient event immediately before the ending.

Prankdom never needs to volunteer that distinction.

The intended community result is a small number of people insisting they saw something that most users have never encountered and that the app itself makes difficult to document through ordinary means.

---

## 19. Seasonal completion events

Selected calendar dates can support special themed completion material.

Initial candidates include:

- Halloween
- April Fools' Day

These may insert unique themed sequences or completion events while retaining **YOU SURVIVED!** as the canonical completed state.

Seasonal events are separate from the undocumented 1-in-1,000 event and can give repeat players a reason to deliberately arrange for a countdown to finish on a particular date.

---

## 20. Accelerated time development model

Seven Days should be developed and tested using the **actual seven-day experience**, not a separately authored shortened version.

The application therefore supports a global time-scale multiplier.

### Production

Production operates at:

**1× time**

One real second equals one Seven Days second. The complete experience lasts seven real days.

### Development / test

The primary development mode operates at:

**1440× time**

There are 86,400 seconds in one day. A seven-minute test requires one complete Seven Days day to pass every 60 real seconds:

**86,400 ÷ 60 = 1,440**

Therefore:

- 1 real second = 1,440 simulated seconds
- 1 real second = 24 simulated minutes
- 60 real seconds = 1 simulated day
- 7 real minutes = the complete seven-day experience

### One experience, two speeds

The seven-minute version is **not** a separate experience.

The same countdown, event system, progression, notification scheduling, glitches, communication events, gifting prompts, completion sequence, seasonal logic, and rare-event logic operate against the same Seven Days clock.

Only the time scale changes.

Conceptually:

`virtualNow = startVirtualTime + (realNow - startRealTime) × timeScale`

Production:

`timeScale = 1`

Development:

`timeScale = 1440`

### Persistence

Accelerated time continues advancing while Seven Days is closed.

The app must therefore not depend on continuously accumulating frame delta time. It stores the appropriate real-world start/reference timestamp and derives current simulated time from actual elapsed wall-clock time.

For example, if Seven Days is closed for 90 real seconds while running at 1440× development speed, reopening it should reveal that approximately 1.5 simulated days have elapsed.

Events whose scheduled virtual times passed while the application was closed are handled according to the same event rules intended for production.

### Purpose

The seven-minute mode allows the entire Seven Days experience to be repeatedly experienced, tuned, debugged, and evaluated from beginning to end.

Once the experience works correctly at 1440×, production changes the time scale to 1× rather than replacing the timing architecture.

---

## 21. What Seven Days is not

Seven Days is a prank, not malware.

It does not damage the device, erase or encrypt data, lock the user out of their phone, prevent ordinary device control, secretly enroll other people, actually threaten anyone, or cause real-world harm.

The experience uses presentation, timing, legitimate authorized phone capabilities, unpredictability, and the user's imagination.

The recipient remains in control of their device.

---

## 22. Governing design rule

Every feature should be judged against one question:

> **Does this make the unexplained countdown more interesting to live with for seven days?**

If it explains the mystery, probably not.

If it creates actual danger, definitely not.

If it makes somebody put their phone back into their pocket thinking:

> **What the fuck was that?**

then it probably belongs in Seven Days.

---

## The experience in one sentence

**Seven Days is a harmless prank that gives you an unexplained seven-day countdown, spends a week making you increasingly suspicious of your own phone, and finally congratulates you for surviving something that was never going to happen.**
